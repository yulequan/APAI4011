Part 1 · Multiple Choice

Q1Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 8

A language model is best described as:

  • A. A probabilistic model that assigns a probability to word sequences, and can therefore predict the next word.
  • B. A set of grammar rules that decides whether a sentence is grammatical.
  • C. A classifier that assigns a topic label to each document.
  • D. A dictionary that stores the meaning of every word.

Answer: A. A probabilistic model that assigns a probability to word sequences, and can therefore predict the next word.

A language model is a probabilistic model of a sequence of words: it defines the joint probability P(w1, …, wn) via the chain rule, so it can score how likely a phrase/sentence is, and it can complete a context by predicting the next word (e.g., “the cat sat on the ___”).

Q2Multiple Choice4.2 · slides 8–9

Using the chain rule of probability, which factorization of P(the cat sat on the mat) is exact (i.e., makes no independence assumption)?

  • A. P(the) · P(cat) · P(sat) · P(on) · P(the) · P(mat)
  • B. P(the) · P(cat | the) · P(sat | the cat) · P(on | the cat sat) · P(the | the cat sat on) · P(mat | the cat sat on the)
  • C. P(the) · P(cat | the) · P(sat | cat) · P(on | sat) · P(the | on) · P(mat | the)
  • D. P(the) · P(cat | the) · P(sat | the cat) · P(on | the cat sat) · P(the | on) · P(mat | the)

Answer: B. P(the) · P(cat | the) · P(sat | the cat) · P(on | the cat sat) · P(the | the cat sat on) · P(mat | the cat sat on the)

The chain rule expresses the joint probability as a product of conditional probabilities, where each word is conditioned on all preceding words. Option A assumes independence, option C conditions only on the immediately previous word (a bigram-style approximation), and option D uses inconsistent histories.

Q3Multiple Choice4.2 · slides 13–14

With a vocabulary of 40,000 words, roughly how many distinct word sequences of length 10 are possible?

  • A. 4 × 105
  • B. 1010
  • C. 1046
  • D. 1063

Answer: C. 1046

Each of the 10 positions can be any of the 40,000 words, so there are 40,00010 = (4 × 104)10 ≈ 1046 possible sequences. This exponential blow-up is why we cannot count all sequences directly and instead use Markov / n-gram approximations.

Q4Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 13

A trigram model has the counts C(the cat) = 200, C(the cat sat) = 50, C(the cat sat on) = 20. Under the maximum likelihood estimate, what is P(sat | the cat)?

  • A. 20 / 50 = 0.4
  • B. 200 / 50 = 4.0
  • C. 50 / 20 = 2.5
  • D. 50 / 200 = 0.25

Answer: D. 50 / 200 = 0.25

For a trigram, P(sat | the cat) = C(the cat sat) / C(the cat) = 50 / 200 = 0.25. The ratio 20 / 50 = 0.4 would be P(on | the cat sat), and raw counts are not probabilities.

Q5Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 15

What trade-off does the Markov assumption make, according to the lecture?

  • A. It increases the number of parameters to estimate, but captures longer context.
  • B. It makes the model non-probabilistic.
  • C. It reduces the number of parameters to estimate, at the cost of modeling capacity.
  • D. It removes the need for a training corpus.

Answer: C. It reduces the number of parameters to estimate, at the cost of modeling capacity.

The lecture states: use only the recent past to predict the next word (e.g., P(mat | the cat sat on the) ≈ P(mat | on the)); this reduces the number of estimated parameters in exchange for modeling capacity.

Q6Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 34

In add-α (Laplace) smoothing for a bigram model, which formula is correct?

  • A. P(wi | wi−1) = (C(wi−1, wi) + α) / (C(wi−1) + α|V|)
  • B. P(wi | wi−1) = C(wi−1, wi) / C(wi−1)
  • C. P(wi | wi−1) = (C(wi−1, wi) + α) / (C(wi−1) + α)
  • D. P(wi | wi−1) = C(wi−1, wi) + α

Answer: A. P(wi | wi−1) = (C(wi−1, wi) + α) / (C(wi−1) + α|V|)

Add α to every bigram count and renormalize: the denominator gains α|V| because α is added to each of the |V| possible next-word counts. Option B is the raw MLE, option C does not renormalize correctly, and option D is not even a probability.

Q7Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 38

A model interpolates: P(wi | wi−2 wi−1) = λ1 · P(wi | wi−2 wi−1) + λ2 · P(wi | wi−1) + λ3 · P(wi). How are λ1, λ2, λ3 typically chosen in the recipe from the lecture?

  • A. Fixed to 1/3 each and never changed.
  • B. Chosen so that every n-gram probability is at most 1.
  • C. Counted directly from the test corpus.
  • D. Estimated on a held-out development (validation) set to maximize the probability of held-out data.

Answer: D. Estimated on a held-out development (validation) set to maximize the probability of held-out data.

The recipe: first estimate the n-gram probabilities on the training set, then fit the λ hyperparameters on a held-out development set, and finally evaluate the chosen model on the test set. The test set is never used for tuning.

Q8Multiple Choice4.2 · slides 27–28

A k-gram model assigns probability 1/|V| to every word at every step (the model is “fine” with observing any word). What is its perplexity on a test corpus?

  • A. 1
  • B. |V|
  • C. |V|2
  • D. 2|V|

Answer: B. |V|

ppl = 2−(1/n) Σ log2(1/|V|) = 2log2|V| = |V|. When the model is equally uncertain about all |V| words at every step, its perplexity equals the vocabulary size — a very weak model.

Q9Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 26

On a test corpus of 4 words, a bigram model assigns probability 0.5 to each gold word. What is its perplexity?

  • A. 0.5
  • B. 1
  • C. 2
  • D. 16

Answer: C. 2

x = −(1/4) × (4 × log2 0.5) = 1, so ppl = 2x = 2. Intuitively, at each step the model is as uncertain as flipping a fair coin between two words.

Q10Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 46

The training data contains “I have to make sure that the cat gets fed”, but never “dog gets fed”. At test time the model must fill in “I forgot to make sure that the dog gets ___”. Why can a neural LM predict “fed” while an n-gram LM cannot?

  • A. The neural LM has memorized the training sentence.
  • B. The neural LM uses a much larger vocabulary.
  • C. The neural LM backs off to unigram probabilities.
  • D. The neural LM uses word embeddings: since e(cat) ≈ e(dog), similar contexts produce similar predictions.

Answer: D. The neural LM uses word embeddings: since e(cat) ≈ e(dog), similar contexts produce similar predictions.

The n-gram model has never seen “dog gets fed”, so its count is zero. The neural LM exploits the embedding similarity of “cat” and “dog” to generalize: the test context is close to the training context in embedding space, so it can predict “fed”.

Q11Multiple Choice4.2 · slide 49

A feedforward neural LM uses window m = 5, embedding size d, hidden size h: x = [e(the); e(cat); e(sat); e(on); e(the)], h = tanh(Wx + b), z = Uh. What are the dimensions of W and U?

  • A. W ∈ ℝh×5d, U ∈ ℝ|V|×h
  • B. W ∈ ℝh×d, U ∈ ℝ|V|×h
  • C. W ∈ ℝh×5d, U ∈ ℝ|V|×d
  • D. W ∈ ℝh×d, U ∈ ℝd×h

Answer: A. W ∈ ℝh×5d, U ∈ ℝ|V|×h

The input concatenates m = 5 embeddings, so x ∈ ℝ5d and W ∈ ℝh×5d. The output layer produces one score per vocabulary word, so z = Uh ∈ ℝ|V| and U ∈ ℝ|V|×h.

Q12Multiple Choice5.1 · slides 4, 7–8

What is the core idea of a recurrent neural network (RNN)?

  • A. Use a different weight matrix at every time step.
  • B. Apply the same weights repeatedly across time steps, passing a hidden state from step to step.
  • C. Average the embeddings of all words in the sentence.
  • D. Slide a fixed-size window over the sentence, like a feedforward neural LM.

Answer: B. Apply the same weights repeatedly across time steps, passing a hidden state from step to step.

RNNs handle variable-length inputs by applying the same weights (W, U, b) at every position: ht = g(W ht−1 + U xt + b), so the hidden state carries information from the past forward.

Q13Multiple Choice5.1 · slide 32

After training an RNN language model, how does one generate text?

  • A. Extract the most frequent sentences from the training corpus.
  • B. Run the network backwards from the end of the sentence.
  • C. Rank candidate sentences with n-gram counts.
  • D. Sample a word from the predicted distribution, then feed the sampled word back as the next step’s input.

Answer: D. Sample a word from the predicted distribution, then feed the sampled word back as the next step’s input.

The lecture: “You can generate text by repeated sampling. Sampled output is next step’s input.” The model predicts a distribution over the next word, you pick one (by sampling or by taking the most likely word), and append it to the context.

Q14Multiple Choice5.1 · slide 10

Why does the RNNLM not rely on the Markov assumption?

  • A. It uses counts instead of probabilities.
  • B. It does not use the chain rule of probability.
  • C. The hidden state ht can (in principle) contain information about all previous words, so no fixed-length context cutoff is needed.
  • D. It conditions only on the immediately previous word.

Answer: C. The hidden state ht can (in principle) contain information about all previous words, so no fixed-length context cutoff is needed.

An RNNLM factorizes P(w1, …, wn) = ∏t P(wt | w1 … wt−1) through hidden states — “No Markov assumption here! Assume hidden states contain all information from the past.” Note that n-gram models also use the chain rule, but with a Markov truncation.

Q15Multiple Choice5.1 · slide 16

An RNNLM has hidden size h, embedding size d and vocabulary size |V|. What is the running time of a forward pass over a sequence of length n?

  • A. O(h × (d + h + |V|))
  • B. O(n × h × (d + h + |V|))
  • C. O(n × (d + h + |V|))
  • D. O(n × h × (d + h))

Answer: B. O(n × h × (d + h + |V|))

Each time step costs O(h(d + h + |V|)): the recurrent update involves W ∈ ℝh×h and U ∈ ℝh×d, and the output layer uses Wo ∈ ℝ|V|×h. Over n steps this gives O(n × h × (d + h + |V|)).

Q16Multiple Choice5.1 · slides 18, 25

In backpropagation through time (BPTT), if time steps k and t are far apart, the gradient ∂Lt / ∂hk:

  • A. Can grow or shrink exponentially with the distance — the gradient exploding / vanishing problem.
  • B. Is exactly zero.
  • C. Becomes a constant independent of the distance.
  • D. Is always smaller than 1.

Answer: A. Can grow or shrink exponentially with the distance — the gradient exploding / vanishing problem.

The gradient is a product of many Jacobian factors; when k and t are far apart, the gradients are very easy to grow or shrink exponentially — this is exactly the gradient exploding / vanishing problem that motivates gradient clipping and LSTM/GRU variants.

Q17Multiple Choice5.1 · slide 59

In an RNNLM, E ∈ ℝ|V|×d holds the input word embeddings and Wo ∈ ℝ|V|×h is the output matrix. What does “weight tying” refer to?

  • A. Sharing W, U, b across time steps (this is only possible when d = h).
  • B. Freezing the embedding matrix during training.
  • C. Merging E and Wo into a single shared matrix when d = h; it works better empirically and is common practice.
  • D. Sharing the hidden state between a forward and a backward RNN.

Answer: C. Merging E and Wo into a single shared matrix when d = h; it works better empirically and is common practice.

Slide 59: if d = h, we can just merge E and Wo — the parameters become θ = {W, U, b, E}. This works better empirically and becomes common practice, except for very large models. (Sharing W across time steps is a property of every RNN, not the “weight tying” trick.)

Part 2 · Multiple Answers (select all that apply)

Q1Multiple Answers4.2 · slide 32

Which of the following are used in the lecture to handle the sparsity (zero-probability) problem of n-gram models? Select all that apply.

  • A. Increasing the n-gram order k, so that contexts become more specific.
  • B. Additive (Laplace) smoothing: add a small amount α to all counts.
  • C. Discounting: redistribute probability mass from observed n-grams to unobserved ones.
  • D. Interpolation: combine different granularities of n-grams (e.g., trigram + bigram + unigram).

Answer: B, C and D.

The three remedies listed in the lecture are additive smoothing, interpolation and discounting. Increasing k makes sparsity worse (that is the problem being addressed), not better.

Q2Multiple Answers4.2 · slides 50–51

Which limitations apply to the feedforward neural language model with a fixed window of m words? Select all that apply.

  • A. The model cannot be trained by gradient descent.
  • B. W grows linearly with the context size m.
  • C. The output is not a probability distribution over the vocabulary.
  • D. The model learns separate patterns for the same context words appearing at different positions.

Answer: B and D.

The lecture’s “Limitations?” list: W scales linearly with the context size m, and the model learns separate patterns for different positions (“sat on” corresponds to different parameters in W depending on where it appears in the window). Better solutions: recurrent NNs and Transformers. Options A and C are false: the model is trained by gradient descent and outputs a |V|-way softmax distribution.

Q3Multiple Answers5.1 · slide 13

Which of the following are advantages of (simple) RNNs listed in the lecture? Select all that apply.

  • A. Can process inputs of any length.
  • B. Model size does not increase for longer input context.
  • C. The recurrent computation is fast and can be parallelized across time steps.
  • D. In theory, the computation at step t can use information from many steps back.

Answer: A, B and D.

Option C describes a disadvantage: recurrent computation is slow (it cannot be parallelized over time steps), which is one motivation for Transformer networks. The lecture also notes that in practice it is difficult to access information from many steps back (an optimization issue), even though this is possible “in theory”.

Q4Multiple Answers5.1 · slides 42–47

Which statements about bidirectional RNNs are correct? Select all that apply.

  • A. A hidden state can combine information from both the left and the right context.
  • B. They are applicable only when the entire input sequence is available.
  • C. They are a good fit for text classification (e.g., concatenate the two final hidden states or pool over all hidden states).
  • D. They are the natural choice for autoregressive text generation.

Answer: A, B and C.

A bidirectional RNN runs one RNN left-to-right and one right-to-left: ht can contain information from x1 … xt and from xt … xn, which is why bidirectionality is powerful when you have the entire input (the key idea behind BERT). For text generation you cannot see the future to predict the next word, so option D is wrong.

Part 3 · True / False

Q1True / False4.2 · slides 8, 20

A language model can be used both to score how likely a given sentence is and to generate text by predicting one word at a time.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: True

The LM defines P(w1, …, wn) via the chain rule (so it can score sentences), and generation uses the estimated distribution to predict one word at a time — choosing the most likely word or sampling from the distribution.

Q2True / False4.2 · slide 17

Using a larger n in an n-gram model always gives a better model in practice, because it captures more context.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: False

Larger n is “more accurate and better — assuming infinite data”. With finite data, most long n-grams are never observed, so the probabilities cannot be estimated reliably (too sparse). This is exactly why we cannot simply keep a very large value of k.

Q3True / False4.2 · slide 25

In intrinsic evaluation, it is acceptable to train the model on part of the test corpus, as long as perplexity is reported.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: False

Intrinsic evaluation must be done on a different, unseen corpus: “Training on any part of test set not acceptable!” A model trained on test data would look artificially good.

Q4True / False4.2 · slides 31–32

If a language model assigns probability zero to some word of the test corpus, its perplexity is undefined (infinite) unless smoothing is applied.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: True

If P(affray | voice doth us) = 0 for some word in the test corpus, then P(test corpus) = 0 and the perplexity is undefined — one reason we need smoothing, which makes sure all probabilities are non-zero.

Q5True / False5.1 · slides 7–8, 13

In a simple RNN, the number of parameters grows with the length of the input sequence.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: False

This is one of the advantages of RNNs: model size does not increase for longer input context. The same weights W, U, b are reused at every time step, so the parameter count is fixed by d and h, regardless of the sequence length.

Q6True / False5.1 · slides 28–29

Gradient clipping is mainly used to solve the vanishing gradient problem.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: False

Gradient clipping (scale the gradient down when its norm exceeds a threshold, before the SGD update) is a solution for gradient exploding. Gradient vanishing is a harder problem to solve, and it motivates advanced RNN variants such as LSTMs and GRUs.

Q7True / False5.1 · slide 30

With truncated backpropagation through time, hidden states are carried forward across chunks, but gradients are back-propagated only for a limited number of steps.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: True

Truncated BPTT runs forward and backward through chunks of the sequence instead of the whole sequence: “carry hidden states forward in time forever, but only back-propagate for some smaller number of steps.” This keeps training affordable for long sequences.

Q8True / False5.1 · slides 27–28

Because of vanishing gradients, an RNNLM may capture the wrong dependency: in “The writer of the books ___ planning a sequel”, it may prefer “are” since the nearer word “books” dominates the signal.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: True

The correct subject “writer” (singular → “is”) is far from the verb, while “books” is close. When gradients vanish, information from many steps back fades, so the model may in effect capture the wrong recent dependency and predict “are” — one of the key motivations for better RNN variants.

Q9True / False5.1 · slides 40–41

Stacking 2–4 RNN layers is common in practice and is usually better than a single layer.

  • T. True
  • F. False

Answer: True

RNNs are already “deep” along the time dimension; stacking layers adds depth in another dimension (the hidden states of one layer feed the next). The lecture notes that 2 to 4 layers is common and usually better than 1 layer; Transformer networks can go up to 24 layers with skip-connections.
